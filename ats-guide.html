How Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) & AI Resume Scoring Work in 2026

Written for HR Managers, Talent Acquisition Leads & Hiring Founders · Reading time: 8 minutes

Every week, corporate talent acquisition teams and startup founders face the same operational bottleneck: a single remote software engineering or data analyst job posting receives between 150 and 600 resumes within days. Reading 200 two-page PDFs manually requires nearly 10 hours of uninterrupted reading time. To solve this without sacrificing fairness, modern HR teams rely on a combination of deterministic Applicant Tracking System (ATS) parsing and Large Language Model (LLM) semantic evaluation.

1. Stage One: Document Parsing & Text Normalization

Before any scoring algorithm can evaluate a candidate, the raw binary file (.pdf or .docx) must be converted into clean, structured UTF-8 text. Legacy ATS platforms often failed when applicants used two-column layouts, tables, or custom icons, causing experienced engineers to be rejected simply because their text streams were scrambled.

Modern browser-based parsers—such as Mozilla's PDF.js for portable documents and Mammoth.js for Word XML archives—extract text tokens page by page, normalizing whitespace and stripping decorative graphics. This ensures that a candidate who writes a clean single-column Word document and a candidate who exports a LaTeX or Canva PDF are judged strictly on their qualifications.

2. Stage Two: Keyword Matching vs. Semantic AI Evaluation

Historically, first-generation resume rankers relied purely on exact string matching (TF-IDF or boolean keyword counts). If a job description asked for "PostgreSQL" and "REST APIs", a candidate who wrote "Postgres database optimization" and "RESTful microservices" might receive a lower score. Furthermore, candidates could game legacy systems by hiding white-text keywords in the footer of their PDF.

How Hybrid Scoring Solves the Keyword Trap

Deterministic ATS Layer

Instantly verifies mandatory hard skills, programming languages, certifications, email addresses, phone numbers, and explicit years of experience.

Contextual LLM Layer (Groq AI)

Reads bullet points under work history to verify whether skills were used in real production environments, summarizing strengths and skill gaps.

3. Understanding the 0–100% Match Score Breakdown

When you upload a batch of resumes into AI Resume Ranker, each applicant receives a composite score from 0% to 100% and is categorized into one of three actionable tiers:

  • Eligible Fit (75% – 100% Match): The candidate satisfies nearly all core technical requirements and meets or exceeds the target experience level. These candidates should be prioritized for your Top 10 interview shortlist.
  • Potential Fit (50% – 74% Match): The candidate possesses strong foundational skills (for example, strong React and JavaScript experience) but is missing 1 or 2 secondary tools (such as Docker or AWS) that could be learned on the job.
  • Not Eligible (<50% Match): The resume targets a different specialization or seniority tier than the role requires.

4. Preventing Bias & Protecting Candidate Privacy

Ethical AI recruitment requires two non-negotiable guardrails: human-in-the-loop review and strict data privacy. Automated ranking should never be used to make a final hiring decision without human oversight; rather, its purpose is to surface the top 10 to 20% of applicants so recruiters can read those resumes thoroughly and conduct structured interviews.

Equally important, resumes contain sensitive personal identifiable information (PII) including home addresses, phone numbers, and employment histories. Our architecture processes text extraction inside the recruiter's browser and performs scoring ephemerally in memory—meaning candidate files are never archived in a database or used to train third-party models.
